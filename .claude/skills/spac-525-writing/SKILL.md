## 0. Core standard (applies to everything)

Everything must be logical, easy to read, easy to follow, on topic, credible, and perfectly cited. Do not make anything complicated.

- Logical: each paragraph makes one claim, and every sentence moves that claim forward in an order a reader can follow without rereading.
- On topic with natural flow (essential): every sentence serves the paragraph's single point, and every paragraph serves the thesis or the question asked. Professor Ramos called out his writing for "whiplash" several times, meaning sentences that jump between unrelated topics. Section 5, item 7 sets the full rule; it applies to every piece of writing, from a one-paragraph peer reply to the term paper.
- Easy to read and follow: plain words, one idea per sentence, named connections between ideas. If a sentence needs a second read, simplify it.
- Credible: only sources that meet section 8, and never a claim larger than the source or the legal text supports.
- Perfectly cited: every factual or legal sentence carries a correct APA 7 citation, and every reference entry is complete and verified.
- Analysis, not summary: a summary of facts or a recital of treaty articles is not graduate writing. Facts and legal text are evidence for an argument. Each paragraph must take a position, use the evidence to support it, and explain what it means (why it matters, what follows, what it changes). If a paragraph could be retitled "Facts about X" or "What Article VI says", rewrite it around a claim.
- Openings: never begin an academic post (or its opening sentence) with "I believe". Avoid the same hedge in "I think", "I feel", and "In my opinion" openers. State the claim directly ("Article VI of the Outer Space Treaty makes the United States answerable for every commercial launch it licenses ..."), and let the evidence carry the authority.

## 1. Hard style rules (no exceptions)

These are his "zero-deduction" rules for ERAU coursework.

- No contractions. Write "does not", "is not", "will not", "it is".
- No em dashes (or en dashes or double hyphens in prose). Use a period, comma, parentheses, or a connector word instead (a semicolon or colon only where section 2 allows it).
- No possessive apostrophes. Use "of" phrases: "the surface of the Moon", "the license of SpaceX", "the jurisdiction of the FAA". Titles in a reference list stay as published.
- Banned vocabulary: delve, elevate, foster, unlock, landscape, underscore, pivotal, testament, beacon, tapestry, realm, garner, vital.
- Never open an academic post with "I believe" (see section 0).
- Zero standard AI text patterns (section 7). This is a hard rule, not a preference, and the checker enforces it.
- APA 7 throughout (section 9): an inline parenthetical citation for every factual or legal claim, plus a full References page. No page or paragraph numbers in in-text citations; paraphrase instead of quoting, because APA requires a page number for a direct quotation.

## 2. Natural flow (top priority after the hard rules)

Everything must sound as close to natural as possible: like a thoughtful graduate student explaining his reasoning to a colleague, never robotic, clipped, or choppy. The hard rules make the writing more formal, so the rest of the prose has to work harder to sound human. Legal writing makes this harder still, so translate legal text into plain reasoning rather than stacking statutory language.

- Read-aloud test. Every sentence should sound like something he could say out loud in a seminar without stumbling. If it sounds like a slide bullet, a legal clause, or a press release, rewrite it.
- Connect every sentence to the one before it and to the paragraph's point. Pick up a word or idea from the previous sentence ("Under that article, ...", "That duty also does not ...") so the paragraph reads as one line of thought, not a stack of separate claims. A sentence that cannot pick up anything from the one before it is usually off topic (see section 5, item 7).
- Vary sentence openings. Do not start consecutive sentences the same way ("The ...", "The ...", "This ..."), and do not open with a label-style "This X" every time.
- Use plain verbs and people-shaped phrasing over stiff nouns: "the United States must authorize and supervise" over "authorization and continuing supervision are required"; "pull against each other" over "create a tension between".
- First person is fine and natural inside discussion posts, peer replies, and responses to comments ("I agree", "what I meant", "you raise a fair point"), but never as an "I believe" opener. Papers stay in third person ("This paper").
- Semicolons: in peer replies and responses to comments, use semicolons minimally, ideally none and never more than one per reply. Use a period or a connector word ("because", "since", "and", "although") instead. The greeting is its own sentence: "Thank you, Dylan. You raise...", never "Thank you, Dylan; you raise...". In papers and primary posts, allow at most one semicolon per paragraph.
- Colons: at most one per paragraph, and do not build sentences out of colon-plus-fragment. Prefer a full clause with a connector.
- No clipped fragments, telegraphic phrasing, or jargon shorthand in prose ("bankable", "de-risk", "CapEx"). Spell the idea out in normal language. Define legal terms of art ("continuing supervision", "launching State", "non-appropriation") the first time they appear.
- Small conversational softeners are welcome in replies when they read naturally: "though", "also", "that said". Do not overuse them.
- When trimming to the word limit, cut a whole redundant phrase or idea; never compress the remaining sentences into clipped, unnatural forms.
- Peer replies and responses to comments sound collegial and conversational within academic standards: acknowledge the person by name, respond to what they actually said, and close on a natural sentence rather than a formula. A reply stays on the classmate's point; new evidence must build on or test that point, not open a separate topic.
- Peer replies take a complimentary, agreeable tone. Compliment something specific and substantive in the classmate's post (their example, their reading of a treaty article, a connection they made), agree where they are right ("I think you are right that ...", "I also agree that ..."), and build on their point as a colleague adding to it. A compliment names what was strong, so it never reads as generic praise.
- The agreeable tone does not remove evidence-based critique. When the evidence or the legal text complicates their point, keep the challenge and frame it as extending their idea ("That raises a question about ...", "Your point may depend on ...") rather than as a correction. Never drop a real problem, including a misread treaty article or statute, or overstate agreement to keep the tone pleasant.

Example (response to a classmate comment).

Robotic: "Thank you, Dylan, for identifying the tension between R2 and R3, because it shows where my recommendation needs a clearer boundary. The deferral applies only to Orbital Reef capital spending ... Your concern also suggests a refinement: release station spending in stages, each tied to a signed private customer."

Natural: "Thank you, Dylan. You raise a fair point about how R2 and R3 pull against each other, and it showed where my recommendation needed clearer limits. What I meant to defer is major Orbital Reef spending, because ... Your concern does point to a better approach, though: releasing station spending in stages as private customers sign."

## 3. His voice

- His natural register is long, technical sentences that carry reasoning in subordinate clauses, with disciplinary precision. Keep that structure. Do not chop his prose into short, punchy sentences; he finds back-to-back short declaratives choppy and repeated identical sentence shapes templated.
- Sentence rhythm variation is good in small doses. Use an occasional shorter sentence to land a point or break a dense stretch, but sparingly and never as a pattern.
- He prefers formal connectors ("Although ...", "Because", "since", "Under that framework") over looser ones ("While ...", "The focus is shifting toward ...").
- Use specifics: numbers, dates, article and section numbers, named programs, spelled-out terms ("Committee on the Peaceful Uses of Outer Space (COPUOS)") rather than vague words like "traditionally" or "significant".
- When he asks for "shorter" or "more concise", remove redundancy and excess complexity; do not restructure his sentences into clipped forms.
- Tone is warm and direct, never smug. He has asked for writing that is "not so smart ass". His natural academic polish is authentic; do not dumb it down. With classmates, the warmth comes through as specific compliments and genuine agreement (see section 2).

## 4. Paragraph rules

- Every paragraph stays under 150 words. That is a cap, not a target. Once the point is made and supported, stop. Shorter paragraphs are fine. Never pad a paragraph to reach a length.
- One idea per paragraph. Open with a topic sentence that states the point (naturalized BLUF, see section 5), then explanation, evidence, and analysis, all about that one point.
- Paragraph-to-paragraph flow: each new paragraph opens by linking to the thesis or to the paragraph before it, so the reader always knows why this point comes next.
- If an idea runs past 149 words, tighten first (see the trimming rule in section 2). Split only if both halves can each stand on their own as a complete point.
- No one-sentence paragraphs, including introductions and conclusions.
- Run `scripts/check_draft.py` to confirm counts and rules before delivering (see workflow).

## 5. Professor Brian Ramos's standards (SPAC 512; apply to all of his writing, including SPAC 525)

From his written feedback on Lisaney's work:

1. Lead with the point (BLUF). State the conclusion first; detail follows for readers who want it. He criticized a 185-word paragraph that amounted to one sentence and should have opened with that sentence.
   - Naturalize the BLUF. The opening sentence carries the main point the way an ordinary academic thesis or topic sentence would. Never label it or write it in memo style ("Bottom line:", "BLUF:", "In short,", a bolded summary line, or a clipped headline-style claim).
   - Memo-style (avoid): "Bottom line: the Outer Space Treaty does not ban space mining."
   - Naturalized (use): "Article II of the Outer Space Treaty bars national appropriation of celestial bodies, yet it leaves open whether extracted resources can be owned, which is the gap the United States filled with its 2015 commercial space statute."
   - In peer replies, the point comes right after the greeting, stated in the complimentary, collegial tone of section 2 rather than as a formal thesis.
2. Before using any table, equation, or figure, say where it comes from and what it is.
3. Explain what numbers mean. A reader who has not read the cited source needs context (he once asked what "1.5 net worth" referred to). The same applies to legal numbers: say what "51 U.S.C. § 51303" or "14 C.F.R. part 450" covers the first time it appears.
4. Set up each topic clearly so strong analysis does not get "lost in the sauce". Lisaney's tendency under pressure is to write more and more; resist it.
5. Never place two ideas side by side without naming the connection between them.
6. Avoid long paragraphs that boil down to a single sentence.
7. No "whiplash" (essential). All writing must stay on topic and flow naturally.
   - Every sentence must connect to the sentence before it and to the paragraph's single point. If a sentence introduces a new treaty, statute, agency, company, number, or argument, the same sentence or the one before it must say how it relates to the point.
   - Delete any sentence that is interesting but not needed for the point, even if it is accurate and cited.
   - Keep one subject in focus per paragraph. Do not alternate between two legal regimes, two eras, or two arguments within a paragraph unless the paragraph is explicitly a comparison and says so in its first sentence.
   - The closing sentence returns to the paragraph's point or the thesis; it never opens a new topic or a teaser for the next paragraph.
   - No claim-then-retreat: once a paragraph or paper takes a position, later sentences must not weaken or contradict it. Claims stay consistent from start to finish.
   - Whiplash test: read only the first sentence of the paragraph, then each later sentence in turn, and ask "does this follow from what came right before, and does it serve the first sentence?" Any "no" means rewrite the link, move the sentence, or cut it.

General ERAU expectations carried over from SPAC 512: discussion posts take an evidence-based position supported by data and multiple sources; replies give evidence-based critique of classmates in a complimentary, agreeable tone; no reuse of his own prior coursework (self-plagiarism).

## 6. Academic writing fundamentals (all academic work)

- Purpose: clear, logical, evidence-based transmission of knowledge that others can evaluate. Not personal opinion.
- Clarity and precision: define key terms once and use them consistently. Use the exact legal name of each instrument and keep it stable.
- Argue, do not just describe. Take a clear position that answers the question directly, supported by reasoning and evidence.
- Structure: a focused introduction that sets purpose and direction; body paragraphs each built on one analytical point; a conclusion that synthesizes rather than repeats.
- Evidence: introduce each source, explain why it is relevant, and engage with its implications. Evidence supports his analysis; it never replaces it.
- Tone: objective, careful, professional. No emotional or informal language, no unsupported claims, no absolute statements. Acknowledge limitations and contested readings of the law.
- Consistency: terminology, tense, citation style, and formatting stay stable throughout.
- Revision order: structure and argument first, topic focus and natural flow second, surface errors last.

## 7. Standard AI text patterns (hard rule: zero allowed)

Zero standard AI text patterns may appear in any deliverable. `scripts/check_draft.py` enforces this list, and anything it flags must be fixed before delivery.

Fix each hit by rewriting the sentence around the actual claim: say what the evidence or legal text shows and why it matters. Never fix a hit by swapping in a synonym ("crucial" to "critical", "moreover" to "also"), because the empty structure stays and the sentence still reads as machine-made.

- Inflated significance: "stands as a testament", "plays a key role", "leaves a lasting impact", "watershed moment", "key turning point", "deeply rooted", "a broader movement".
- Promotional tone: "rich heritage", "breathtaking", "groundbreaking", "cutting-edge", "enduring legacy", "game-changer". Describe; do not sell.
- Editorial filler: "it is important to note", "it is worth mentioning", "no discussion would be complete without", "in this article/post".
- Weak opinion openers: "I believe", "I think", "I feel", "In my opinion" at the start of a post or paragraph.
- Labeled or memo-style BLUF openers: "Bottom line:", "BLUF:", "The key takeaway is", "Simply put," (see section 5).
- Recap endings: "In conclusion", "Overall", "In summary", "In short", or restating everything at the end.
- Formula sections like "Challenges and Future Outlook" that say challenges exist but the future is bright.
- AI word bank (beyond his banned list): intricate, crucial, showcase, enhance, vibrant, boast, align with, seamless, robust, navigate (figurative), multifaceted, ever-evolving, holistic, additionally, sharpens, leverage.
- "Serves as", "stands as", or "boasts" where "is" or "has" works.
- Overused transitions: moreover, furthermore, in addition, on the other hand. Name the actual logical relationship instead.
- "Not X, but Y" or "it is not just X; it is Y" negation, including the two-sentence version.
- Trailing -ing clauses that add shallow analysis: "..., highlighting", "..., ensuring", "..., reflecting", "..., underscoring".
- Reflexive groups of three adjectives, examples, or clauses.
- Synonym cycling to avoid repeating a noun (the treaty, the accord, the instrument, the framework). Repeat the precise term.
- False ranges: "from the Space Race to the commercial era".
- Vague attribution: "experts say", "some scholars argue", "industry reports suggest", "observers note". Name the source and cite it.
- Generic formula praise in replies: "Great point!", "Great post!", "I really enjoyed". Compliments stay, but they must name the specific thing that was strong.
- Formatting tells in prose deliverables: unnecessary bold, bullet points with bolded labels, emojis, title case on every heading.
- Chatbot leftovers: "Certainly!", "I hope this helps", "Great question", knowledge-cutoff disclaimers.

The checker catches the word- and phrase-level patterns. Groups of three, synonym cycling, two-sentence negations, and formula sections need a read-through, so check them by eye in workflow step 7.

## 8. Sources

### General rule

- Every reference must be scholarly, peer-reviewed, and directly relevant to the claim and to the module (section 5b).
- Every reference must have a working, verifiable link to the full text with no paywall (open access, an author or institutional repository copy of the published version, or a DOI that resolves to free full text). Confirm the full text actually opens before citing.
- Sources must be current and substantive. Prefer recent scholarship unless the point is historical, and re-verify any date or status that can change.
- Read the full text of every source used. Never cite from an abstract, search snippet, or memory. Never cite a source for more than it actually says.
- Verify every citation element (authors, year, title, journal, volume, issue, pages, DOI, URL) against the source itself. Never invent a URL or DOI; find the real one.
- If a claim cannot be supported by a source that meets this standard, narrow the claim or cut it.
- If an uploaded file does not meet the standard, say so and suggest a stronger replacement rather than citing it quietly.

### Exception a: primary legal and policy sources (allowed freely)

Space law depends on primary texts, so these may be cited freely:

- International space treaties and agreements: the Outer Space Treaty (1967), the Rescue Agreement (1968), the Liability Convention (1972), the Registration Convention (1975), and the Moon Agreement (1979).
- UN General Assembly resolutions and COPUOS documents (including principles, guidelines, and reports).
- The Artemis Accords (a non-binding set of bilateral political commitments, not a treaty; describe it accurately).
- U.S. statutes, for example Title 51 of the U.S. Code and public laws.
- Federal regulations: the Code of Federal Regulations and the Federal Register (for example FAA and FCC rules).
- Court decisions.
- Executive orders and presidential directives (including Space Policy Directives).
- Official agency policy documents from NASA, FAA, FCC, NOAA, the Department of State, and the Department of Commerce.

Conditions for every primary legal or policy source:

- Link to the official free text: UNOOSA (unoosa.org), the UN Treaty Collection or UN documents system, govinfo.gov, ecfr.gov, federalregister.gov, congress.gov, uscode.house.gov, or the issuing agency or court.
- Cite the current version, or the version in force at the time being discussed. Check for amendments, recodifications, superseded rules, and withdrawn or revoked directives before citing. For the eCFR, note the "up to date as of" date; for statutes, check whether the section was amended after the edition cited.
- Primary legal sources never replace scholarship. Every primary post must still include at least one peer-reviewed source that interprets or analyzes the law being discussed.

### Exception b: SPAC 525 textbook and assigned readings (used sparingly)

- The required textbook and assigned readings may be cited, at most one per post or reply.
- Never cite them alone; always pair them with peer-reviewed sources.
- Link only to an authorized full-text copy (the publisher, the ERAU library or course shell when he confirms that is the source, or an open copy posted by the rights holder). Never link to pirated uploads or shadow libraries. If no authorized link exists, cite the book in APA 7 without a link and tell him.
- Ask him for the textbook details (author, edition, year, publisher) instead of guessing; once known, add them to section 5a.

### Excluded from the reference list

News articles, blogs, law firm client alerts, trade press, Wikipedia, and paywalled databases such as Westlaw, Lexis, and HeinOnline. These may be used for research, to find a lead, or to flag a risk to him, but the cited source must be a free official text or an open-access scholarly version.

## 9. APA 7 citations, legal references, and file formatting

### In-text citations

- Parenthetical citations in correct APA 7 form, with no page or paragraph numbers. Paraphrase instead of quoting.
- Use "&" inside parentheses and "and" in narrative citations; use "et al." for three or more authors from the first citation.
- Legal designators that identify the law itself (a treaty article, a U.S.C. or C.F.R. section) are part of the legal citation, not page numbers, and may appear when the sentence turns on that provision. Prefer naming the article or section in the sentence ("Article VI of the Outer Space Treaty ...") and keeping the parenthetical to name and year.

### Legal references (APA 7, Chapter 11)

Format treaties, statutes, regulations, cases, and executive orders per the legal reference rules in Chapter 11 of the APA Publication Manual (7th ed.), which follow Bluebook conventions. Check each entry against the manual pattern before delivery, and when a source type is unusual, tell him which pattern was used.

| Source type | Reference list pattern | In-text |
|---|---|---|
| Treaty | Name of Treaty, Month Day, Year, Treaty Source. URL | (Name of Treaty, Year) |
| Statute (session law) | Name of Act, Pub. L. No. xxx-xxx, Volume Stat. Page (Year). URL | (Name of Act, Year) |
| Statute (code) | Name of Act, Title U.S.C. § Section (Year). URL | (Name of Act, Year) |
| Federal regulation (C.F.R.) | Title of Regulation, Volume C.F.R. § Section (Year). URL | (Title of Regulation, Year) |
| Federal Register rule | Title of Rule, Volume Fed. Reg. Page (Month Day, Year) (to be codified at Volume C.F.R. pt. x). URL | (Title of Rule, Year) |
| Court decision | Name v. Name, Volume Reporter Page (Court Year). URL | (*Name v. Name*, Year) |
| Executive order | Exec. Order No. xx,xxx, Volume Fed. Reg. Page (Year). URL | (Exec. Order No. xx,xxx, Year) |
| UN resolution | Title, G.A. Res. Number, U.N. Doc. A/RES/Number (Month Day, Year). URL | (G.A. Res. Number, Year) |
| Agency policy document | Agency. (Year). *Title* (Report No. if any). URL | (Agency, Year) |

Worked examples (verify every element and the URL against the official source before using):

- Treaty on Principles Governing the Activities of States in the Exploration and Use of Outer Space, Including the Moon and Other Celestial Bodies, January 27, 1967, 18 U.S.T. 2410, 610 U.N.T.S. 205. URL from UNOOSA
- Exec. Order No. 13,914, 85 Fed. Reg. 20381 (2020). URL from federalregister.gov
- Case names are italicized in the reference list and in text; treaty, statute, and regulation names are not italicized.

## 11. Accuracy audit (every response)

Before delivering, audit the response for hallucinations, factual errors, and over-reaches, then post a short audit note in chat (never in the submission file).

- Every factual claim traces to a source that was actually read and that says what the sentence says.
- No number, date, name, title, or quotation is invented or carried over from memory without checking.
- No claim is stretched beyond the source (for example "proves" where the source "suggests").
- For legal content, also check that:
  - every treaty article, statute section, regulation, and case holding is quoted or described correctly;
  - each provision is current, or correctly dated to the period discussed (amendments, recodification, revocation, withdrawals, and party status checked);
  - nothing is stretched beyond what the text or the holding actually says (for example, the Artemis Accords are non-binding commitments, the Moon Agreement does not bind the United States, which is not a party, and a court holding is not extended past its facts).
- The audit note lists what was checked and anything corrected, narrowed, or left uncertain. Flag uncertainty instead of hiding it.
